# v0.18 plan — the modern valley: clean air, new industries, your neighbourhood, landmarks, the world market

Research and sources: `docs/V018-RESEARCH.md`.

## Where the game stands (v0.17.1)
- **Environmental law**:
  - Smoke Control (1946, can move between 1935 and 1960) clears the valley's smoke but asks nothing of your works.
  - The Clean Water Act (1972) forbids "dump", and from 1965 dumping risks fines.
  - Two Clairton cards (1972 decree, 1975 crisis) appear only with by-product coke ovens.
  - From 1980, Superfund bills you for past dumping.
  - The only pollution choice is the waste switch (dump / standard / abate).
  - Missing:
    - the early ordinances;
    - the county law of 1949;
    - the Clean Air Act and EPA (1970);
    - nonattainment limits on expansion (1977);
    - real control equipment;
    - consent decrees as a process;
    - inversion emergencies that demand a curtailment plan;
    - the 1981 "stretch-out".
- **Modernisation**:
  - Renaissance I and II clear the Point and add towers.
  - The airport, Shippingport, the arena, the stadium and Heinz Hall appear on their dates.
  - Steel automation arrives in 1982.
  - The industries that replaced steel don't exist, and the downtown towers belong to no one.
- **Neighbourhoods**: company housing, endowing a church or hospital, founding great works early, URA clearance. There's nothing for the streets around your own works.
- **Landmarks**: ~60 dated, mostly libraries, colleges and the big venues. Few hospitals, high schools, neighbourhood parks or the named downtown towers.
- **The world market**: one line of code. From 1960 a steady price cut (steel −2.5% a year, metalworks −1.5%, glass −1%, floor 45%). No effect on how much is bought, no trade policy, no dollar, no recessions, no minimills.

## A. Clean air and water as a fight, not a switch
1. **Laws**: add to the politics law table, each with a historical year and a range the elections can move:
   - Early smoke ordinances (1869, 1892) and the Bureau of Smoke Regulation (1908): "symbolic" with tiny fines.
   - City smoke ordinance (1911) and the Mellon Institute smoke study (1911–14) event.
   - Purity of Waters (1905) and Clean Streams (1937; 1945 and 1965 amendments).
   - Allegheny County air law (1949, after Donora).
   - County air rules (1970).
   - Clean Air Act and EPA (1970).
   - Clean Air Act amendments (1977).
   - Steel Compliance Extension (1981).
2. **Control equipment**: added to every industry's process list, with cost rising steeply the cleaner you go (the 1979 Battelle curve: 90% / 97% / 99% removal).
   - Smokeless firing and stokers (1908+)
   - Electrostatic precipitators (1910+)
   - Coke-oven door seals and quench-water treatment (1960s)
   - Furnace hoods and baghouses
   - Scrubbers (1970s)
   - Water treatment (1950s+)
   Each lowers your smoke and outfall and costs upkeep. Laws set the level you must reach.
3. **Consent decrees**: when a law catches you short you choose:
   - Comply now.
   - Sign a decree: a deadline of 4–10 years with weak interim limits. Violations pile up, and the decree can collapse into lawsuits and daily fines, as Clairton's did in 1972–75.
   - Defy: fines, reputation, and a GASP campaign from 1969.
   The 1981 stretch-out lets steel put the cleanup money into modernisation instead.
4. **Inversions**: Donora (1948) and November 1975 as valley-wide emergencies.
   - From 1970, a works without a curtailment plan is fined and loses reputation.
   - Deaths go in the ledger.
   - Banking furnaces costs output.
5. **Expansion permits after 1977**: in a nonattainment valley, a new furnace or expansion needs offsets (clean up an old unit first).
6. **Data fixes**:
   - Donora deaths: 20.
   - Clairton: the 1972 decree, its collapse in 1975, and the $100M programme (1974). The "1979 decree" is unconfirmed: keep it only as a game decision, labelled as such.

## C. Your neighbourhood
A **Neighbourhood** card for the district your works sits in (and each works you own), with five measures:
- **Health**: infant deaths, typhoid, smoke.
- **Housing**: crowding, lodgers, repair.
- **Amenity**: baths, library, playground, paving.
- **Cohesion**: churches, halls, settlement houses.
- **Land value**.

They move labour, morale, turnover, strike support and who settles there, and the streets show it (paving, gardens, repainted houses, or decay).

Investments, in their historical windows:

| Investment | From | Effect, and the catch |
|---|---|---|
| Paving, sewers and water mains (lobby the borough or pay) | 1850 | Health ++. Filtered water (1908 city, earlier if you pay) cut typhoid ~85% |
| Bath house, library and gym (the Carnegie model) | 1888 | Amenity, morale. Built beside a strike ground, it's read as penance |
| Playground (Civic Club, 1896) | 1896 | Health, children; parents may want trade classes instead |
| Settlement house (Kingsley, Soho) | 1893 | Cohesion, schooling; reformers watch your works |
| Company hospital, relief fund | 1890s | Labour ++. Workmen's comp (1915) takes over part of it |
| Company housing for sale (Vandergrift, Aliquippa "Plans") | 1895 | Loyalty and labour peace. Housing by ethnic "Plan" weakens union cohesion (a ledger mark) |
| Parklets and clean-up (Allegheny Conference) | 1944 | Amenity |
| Clearance through the URA | 1946 | Land value up; displacement, a lasting grievance, and the neighbourhood shrinks (Lower Hill 1956) |
| Revolving restoration fund (PHLF) | 1966 | Housing without displacement |
| Rehab lending (Neighborhood Housing Services) | 1968 | Housing, cohesion; needs banks (Mellon, PNC) |
| Community development corporation | 1980 | Slows decline after a closure (Mon Valley Initiative, 1988, in the epilogue) |

## E. The world market and the collapse
The squeeze came mostly from **volume, not price**:
- Shipments went from 111M tons (1973) to 62M (1982).
- Utilisation fell from 92% (1974) to 48% (1982), against a break-even of about 70%.
- Imports rose from 2% (1950s) to 26% (1984).
- The US list price kept rising to 1981 and only then fell about 12%.

The single price line becomes six linked parts:

1. **Demand index by industry and year** (1973 = 100 for steel):
   - growth of about 2.5% a year to 1973;
   - booms in 1974 and 1979;
   - recessions in 1975, 1980 and **1982 (58)**;
   - a partial recovery to 72 in 1984–85;
   - a structural drift of about −1% a year after 1973 (cars 3,300 → 2,300 lb of steel, aluminium cans from 1965, plastics).

   It sets how much you can sell. Unsold capacity idles, and **profit follows utilisation**: below ~70% the works loses money, as the big producers did in 1982 at −$95 a ton.
2. **Import share**: a base curve plus a dollar term, minus trade policy.
   - Base curve: 2% before 1959; the 1959 strike opens the door; 10% by 1965; ~17% by 1971; 12–13% during the 1973–74 shortage; ~18% by 1977–78.
   - The trade-weighted dollar rises 77% from 1980 to 1985 and pushes the share to 26% in 1984.
   - Trade policy cuts it: voluntary restraints (1969–74), trigger prices (1978–82), the US–EC deal (1982), and the 1984 restraints at 18.5% (Steel Import Stabilization Act).

   Imports take their share of the market before you do.
3. **US price vs world price**:
   - The US price is administered and sticky: it rises with costs, and Kennedy faces down U.S. Steel's $6 rise in 1962.
   - Imports land at the world price plus $44–54 a ton in freight and duty.
   - Protection lifts the US price 1–3.5%.
   - By 1984 the gap is ~$90 a ton.
4. **Your cost against the world's**: shown in the Works tab.
   - US labour cost per ton outruns productivity: $265 against Japan's $144 a ton in 1982, with wages 64% over manufacturing.
   - The player closes the gap with:
     - the oxygen furnace (from 1955; Japan 80% by 1970, U.S. Steel only from 1964);
     - continuous casting (from 1965; US 21% vs Japan 71% in 1982);
     - automation;
     - wage and work-rule concessions (a union fight, in the ledger).
   - A works below the world cost keeps its orders.
5. **Minimills**: scrap-fed electric furnaces (Nucor: 2 man-hours a ton against 6–8) take 0 → 16% of the domestic market from 1970 to 1985, in bar, rod and light shapes first. The player can build one (electric-arc process, from 1969) at a fraction of the men.
6. **Other industries**:
   - **Machinery, barges and bridges** follow steel's capital spending a few years late. Mesta goes bankrupt in 1983 and the Dravo yard closes in 1982.
   - **Glass**: float glass from 1963 retires plate lines (the last in 1973); demand follows the car makers.
   - **Electrical equipment**: heavy-apparatus orders shrink and production moves south (turbine headquarters to Orlando in 1982–83; East Pittsburgh closes in 1988).
   - **Aluminium**: the price is set on the London Metal Exchange from 1978, halves from 1980 to 1982, and suffers energy shocks in 1973 and 1979.
   - **Coal chemicals**: follow blast-furnace output; compliance costs from 1972.

The collapse as it happened:
- Carrie Furnaces go cold (1978).
- J&L's Pittsburgh properties are sold (1981).
- Dravo's yard closes (1982).
- Mesta goes bankrupt (1983).
- Duquesne is announced (Dec 1983) and closes (1984).
- Unemployment peaks above 17% (Jan 1983); Beaver County reaches 27%.
- Clairton goes from about 7,000 men to 1,100.

Rival closures follow the model, not fixed dates, but land near history unless the player changes it (buys, saves, or undercuts a works).

**Survival**:
- A modernised, flat-rolled, integrated works with continuous casting by about 1980 and a labour cost near Japan's survives, as Edgar Thomson, Irvin and Clairton did.
- It survives smaller: 60–70% utilisation and a far smaller workforce. U.S. Steel went from 75,000 to 20,000 men.
- The epilogue counts the jobs you kept.
