# Bosses, Strikes and Smokestacks Shape Pittsburgh's Century

The five research notes yield **311 new, deduplicated Pittsburgh-region events from 1850 to 1985** that are not already among the 93 events in Mill Town's `EVENTS.csv`. They cover company foundings and closures, strikes and killings, machine politics and reform, parks, Carnegie libraries, universities, transit, mine disasters and public health. **104 are rated High** (date and season confirmed in a source the researchers read). **185 are Medium**, mostly because the source gives only the year or because they come from the politics notes, which were written without verifying page text. **22 are Low** (background knowledge only). The events cluster tightly around the game's theme. **Industrial money built the city's civic institutions**: Carnegie's mill-town libraries, Magee's trolley-served zoo, Mellon's Kennywood and research institute. **Labor violence peaked whenever private or deputized police were legal** (Morewood 1891, Braddock 1916, Brackenridge 1919, Ambridge 1933), and **New Deal politics, much of it tested in Aliquippa, turned that order upside down**. The research also turns up **several corrections to the existing game file**. The biggest are that Braddock, not Allegheny, opened the first Carnegie library; that Carnegie's 1901 sale price is better given as about $492 million; and that the "Jones & Laughlin" works closing in 1985 belonged by then to LTV Steel. Below are the master table, an analysis of how the events cluster, and the corrections.

## Reading the master table: conventions and confidence

Seasons follow the game's rule: Spring is March to May, Summer is June to August, Autumn is September to November, and Winter is December to February. **January and February count as the previous year's Winter**, so the February 1865 iron wage contract is filed as 1864 Winter. Confidence uses the three-tier scheme requested. **High** means the date and season were confirmed in a source the researchers read. **Medium** means the source gives only the year (so the season shown is the most plausible guess), the sources conflict, or the item comes from the politics notes with a matching search-result source. **Low** means background knowledge only; check these before use. Events already in `EVENTS.csv` were removed even when worded differently. Examples are the 1863 hilltop forts, the 1920 KDKA broadcast, the 1948 Donora smog, the 1959 strike, the 1968 riots, Allegheny City's annexation and the Pittsburgh Survey. Several notes-file items closely tied to existing events (the 1919 Duquesne meeting ban, the 1959 strike settlement, Carnegie's 1883 Homestead purchase) were folded away rather than repeated. Joseph Barker's election as mayor from a jail cell (January 1850, so Winter 1849 by the game's rule) falls just before the game's Spring 1850 start, so it is left out of the table but would make good backstory flavor ([WPXI](https://www.wpxi.com/archive/this-day-january-8-1850-joseph-barker-elected-mayor-while-county-jail/72324R4BBFBZ5C3AOQMZBVWJLI)).

## Money, muscle and mandates: how the events cluster around the game's theme

### Philanthropy follows the payroll, and politics follows the trolley line

The civic-institution events are not a separate track from industry; they are the same money moving through a second channel. Carnegie dedicated the **first Carnegie library in America at Braddock in March 1889**, with a bathhouse in the basement so millworkers could wash before reading ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braddock_Carnegie_Library)). In **November 1898** he opened a $300,000 library and athletic club on the hill above the Homestead Works, six years after the strike ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Library_of_Homestead)). The parks follow the same logic through politics. Boss Christopher Magee offered the city a zoo **on condition it sit at the end of his trolley line**, and the mayor then signed ordinances favorable to Magee's traction interests. Mellon's streetcar company bought Kenny's Grove to create Kennywood as a destination for riders ([Pittsburgh Magazine](https://www.pittsburghmagazine.com/?p=193059)). For game design, this suggests that civic events should be triggered by, or cost, the player's industrial standing rather than float free. A "gift a library" or "build a trolley park" choice could raise worker goodwill or rider revenue while carrying the Homestead-style irony the sources document.

### Private police set the death toll; New Deal law ended the killing era

The labor events show one stark pattern. **Fatal confrontations clustered in the decades when companies could deputize their own police**, from the 1865–66 Coal and Iron Police acts to Pinchot's refusal to renew commissions around 1931 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal_and_Iron_Police)). In that window come Morewood (9 dead, 1891), the Westmoreland strike and Yukon (1910), the Battle of Braddock (3 dead, 1916), Fannie Sellins (1919) and John Barkoski (1929) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_worker_deaths_in_United_States_labor_disputes); [Union Progress](https://www.unionprogress.com/2023/05/05/two-strikes-rattled-the-region-in-1916-one-ended-in-cheers-another-in-bloodshed/)). The turning point was legal, not industrial. Section 7(a) in 1933, the Wagner Act in 1935, and the **April 1937 Supreme Court ruling in the Aliquippa case** shifted power to industrial unions ([Aliquippa borough](https://aliquippapa.gov/2024/04/12/87th-anniversary-of-the-supreme-court-of-the-united-states-decision-in-national-labor-relations-board-vs-jones-laughlin-steel-corp-april-12-1937/)). After that, conflict moved into bargaining, Cold War union splits (CIO expels UE, 1949), and the 1973 no-strike agreement ([Pitt ULS](https://exhibit.library.pitt.edu/labor_legacy/LaborColdWar.html); [Beyond Steel](https://beyondsteel.briem.com/timeline.html)). This argues for a "company police" policy that the player can use until about 1931 and that political events can revoke, with a rising backlash cost each time it produces a death.

### Machines, reformers and authorities trade control of the city

Political control passed through three distinct regimes. The **Magee–Flinn Republican ring** ran from the 1880s to 1901, meshing contracts with Flinn's paving firm. Reformers then struck back with Steffens's exposé, Guthrie's fusion victory, the 1910 graft prosecutions and the 1911 small-council charter. These items come from the politics notes and are rated Medium or Low ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Magee_(politician)); [Steffens](https://observatory.wiki/Classics:The_Shame_of_the_Cities/Pittsburg:_A_City_Ashamed)). The **1933–36 Democratic realignment** under David Lawrence came next, followed by the authority-driven Renaissance: URA in 1946, the 1947 Pittsburgh Package, and the Port Authority in 1956, which took over transit in 1964 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_Regional_Transit)). Finally, the Black Construction Coalition, Flaherty's anti-patronage "Nobody's Boy" campaign and the radical clergy of 1984–85 challenged that order. The notable takeaway is that **public authorities replaced private monopolies** in transit, sewage and even university finance (Pitt became state-related in 1966). That makes "public takeover" a recurring late-game political lever.

### Environmental politics turns jobs against health

The Clairton sequence is the clearest single case of the game's theme. A 1972 consent decree was followed by a 1973 suit over 241 violations and a 1975 air crisis that the EPA linked to 14 deaths. U.S. Steel answered with ads warning that it "can't make steel in Pittsburgh without coke from Clairton. Period." Then came the 1985 benzene spill and the near-bankruptcy of the town itself ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clairton_Coke_Works)). The sequence pairs naturally with the existing Donora and smoke-control events. Smoke enforcement after 1946 also hit working-class households hardest, because cheap bituminous coal was what they could afford ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Pittsburgh)).

## Conclusion

The research supports a game in which **philanthropy, policing and public authority work as levers**, not decoration. The same Pittsburgh fortunes that built mills also built libraries, zoos, research institutes and universities, and the same state that licensed private police later abolished them. A New Deal case from a single Aliquippa mill rewrote national labor law. The richest seams for new mechanics are the Clairton jobs-versus-health sequence (1972–85), the Magee–Flinn machine with its trolley-park patronage (1880s–1901), and the 1960s–80s shift from private monopolies to public authorities. Each is backed by a run of dated, mostly High-confidence events.

The main remaining weakness is the politics layer. Nearly all political events between the Civil War and the 1960s rest on notes written without verified page text. Before shipping, it is worth confirming the Ripper Act, the 1911 charter date, the Lawrence-era authorities and the Steel Valley Authority's founding month. The handful of Low-rated industrial and labor items (Union Switch & Signal, the UMWA strikes of 1894, 1897 and 1922, Dorothy Six, Frick Park) also need checking. A short verification pass on the roughly 70 Medium politics items and 22 Low items would raise most of the table to the same standard as the labor and infrastructure rows.
